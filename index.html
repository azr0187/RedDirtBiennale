Who we are

The Camp Hill RED DIRT BIENNALE is an open-air day long biennale set for late Fall 2026 engaging multiple artists at multiple sites throughout the historically significant town of Camp Hill Alabama. The goal of the event is to produce uniquely challenging, personal works that invite both artist and audience to consider questions of personal and political identity, what a rooted versus un-rooted culture can look like (and un-rooted can cost) in our current cultural landscape, along with the inspiration and challenges that have centered on this largely minority, farm-based community in rural Alabama.

Spanning numerous sites (some more draped with kudzu than others) the RED DIRT BIENNALE sprang from the experience of a small group of artists and science communicators. Six locations throughout Camp Hill have been identified as future sites for artworks, immersive experiences, and community gathering spaces, with the intention being for each space to be curated by the artists engaged there. In addition to supporting musical performances as well as local makers and farmers, the hope is that each artist will leave behind a permanent intervention - both as a gift to the town, as well as a marker and recording of past and future histories.

Historic Significance Of Camp Hill

The town of Camp Hill stands uniquely in both Alabama and U.S. history as the site of the largest ever African American labor organization to occur in American history. As the 1930’s birthplace of the Mighty Sharecroppers Union - (at its largest almost 8000 strong) community members from Camp Hill worked alongside members of the Communist Party of America, with several citizens of this small Alabama town traveling as far as Moscow to train in labor organizing - resulting in a self-led, self-determined campaign for economic justice that some modern historians now propose as the real birthplace of the modern Civil Rights movement.

Combined with being a small town in rural Alabama that found itself as home to New York Universalist Preacher Lyman Ward ( a man of peace and education) who traveled down from the North to meet Booker T. Washington (and later traveled with him on combined speaking tours), as well as being the birthplace of Progressive Senator Claude Pepper (who soon came to be known as “Red” Pepper for his own presumed communist leanings) Camp Hill exists at a nexus of movements and philosophies that might seem unusual in the very Deep South. With a diverse, racially mixed population of a space that still seems never wholly conquered - Camp Hill exists as a town with a history that seems strongly relevant, again. A place on the brink of something great, perhaps still waiting for that Great Leap Forward.

The Spectacle

Among the sites selected as venues are (all in conjunction with local fire departments, and with event-insurance in tow)

  • An abandoned railroad depot, at what was once one of the largest shipping-departure points in all the Southeast for raw cotton
  • An abandoned field that once was home to a large 1970’s mobile-home community
  • An unrestored gas station / garage that housed the first show (now become the art experiment-station Power + Light)
  • A large, historic church in the center of town built by a Universalist preacher from New York State who decided one day to travel down south and ended up partnering with Booker T. Washington
  • The small-town community Library, that sits right next to the railroad tracks
  • The abandoned former public elementary school grounds, more than half of which burned up in a giant conflagration, seven years back.
